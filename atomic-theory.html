The Atomic Theory

From an argument about cutting cheese to a cloud of probability.

Ten people worked out what everything is made of.

Nobody has ever seen an atom with their eyes. The structure below was reasoned out of glowing gas, scattered particles, falling oil drops and equations — one correction at a time. Pick a year to start reading.

~400 BC

Democritus

Abdera, Greece

Argued that if you keep halving a piece of matter you must eventually reach something that cannot be cut. He called it atomos — uncuttable. Everything, he said, is these particles plus empty space between them.

No model It was philosophy, not experiment. He had no way to test it, and Aristotle's rival four-element theory won the argument for the next two millennia.

1803

John Dalton

Manchester, England

Turned the idea into chemistry. Measuring how elements combine, he found they always do so in fixed whole-number ratios — which only makes sense if each element is made of identical particles with a characteristic weight.

Solid spheres Atoms are tiny, hard, indivisible balls. Every atom of an element is identical; different elements have different weights. Reactions just rearrange them — atoms are never created or destroyed.

1897

J. J. Thomson

Cavendish Laboratory, Cambridge

Bent a cathode ray with magnets and electric fields and measured how much it bent. The answer meant the ray was made of particles almost two thousand times lighter than a hydrogen atom — so atoms have parts, and Dalton's solid sphere was wrong.

Plum pudding Negative electrons sit scattered through a diffuse ball of positive charge, like currants in a cake. The positive charge balances the negative, so the whole atom is neutral.

1909

Robert Millikan

University of Chicago

Sprayed oil droplets between two charged plates and tuned the voltage until a droplet hung motionless, gravity exactly balanced by the electric pull. Every charge he measured was a multiple of one small value — the charge on a single electron.

No model He measured rather than pictured. But pairing his charge with Thomson's charge-to-mass ratio gave the electron's actual mass, which turned it from an inference into a known object.

1911

Ernest Rutherford

University of Manchester

Fired alpha particles at gold foil only a few hundred atoms thick. Almost all sailed straight through, but a tiny fraction bounced back — which, in a pudding of spread-out charge, should have been impossible.

Nuclear atom All the positive charge and nearly all the mass are packed into a minute core. Electrons occupy the huge empty volume around it. The atom is mostly nothing.

1913

Niels Bohr

Copenhagen

Rutherford's atom should have collapsed: a circling electron radiates energy and spirals in. Bohr fixed it by decree — only certain orbits are allowed, and an electron in one of them simply does not radiate. The rule reproduced hydrogen's spectral lines exactly.

Planetary, with quantised shells Electrons orbit the nucleus at fixed energy levels. Light is emitted or absorbed only when an electron jumps between levels, and its colour is set by the size of the gap.

1924

Louis de Broglie

Paris

Light had already been shown to act as both wave and particle. In his doctoral thesis, de Broglie proposed the reverse: matter does too, and any particle has a wavelength set by its momentum.

No model of his own But it explained Bohr's arbitrary rule. An orbit is allowed only when the electron's wave closes on itself in a whole number of wavelengths; any other orbit cancels itself out. This became the basis for Schrödinger's equation two years later.

1925–1927

Werner Heisenberg

Göttingen and Copenhagen

Built matrix mechanics, the first complete quantum theory, by refusing to talk about anything unobservable — including electron orbits. In 1927 he showed why: position and momentum cannot both be pinned down, and the sharper you fix one, the blurrier the other becomes.

No model — a limit on all models The uncertainty principle isn't a flaw in the instruments. It says a well-defined orbit is not a thing an electron can have, which rules out Bohr's neat circles for good.

1926

Erwin Schrödinger

Zurich

Took de Broglie's matter waves seriously and wrote the equation they obey. Solving it for hydrogen produced Bohr's energy levels automatically — no special rules required — and it works for atoms Bohr's model could never handle.

Quantum mechanical model An electron has no path. The equation gives an orbital: a region of space with a probability of finding the electron there. The shapes of those orbitals are what determine how atoms bond.

1932

James Chadwick

Cavendish Laboratory, Cambridge

Nuclei weighed about twice what their charge suggested. Chadwick bombarded beryllium, caught radiation that no electric or magnetic field would bend, and showed from the recoils it produced that it was a neutral particle with roughly a proton's mass.

No new model — the missing piece The neutron accounted for the extra mass and explained isotopes: same element, same charge, different number of neutrons. It also made nuclear fission possible to imagine, since a neutral particle can enter a nucleus unopposed.

What the sequence shows

No one on this list was simply right. Each model was the best account of the evidence available, and each was replaced when an experiment produced something it could not explain — a bent cathode ray, a rebounding alpha particle, a nucleus that weighed too much.

The current model is not the end of the list either. Protons and neutrons turned out to have parts of their own, and quarks were confirmed in the 1970s.
